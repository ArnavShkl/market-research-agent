[Slide 1 | 0:00–0:19]
Imagine you’re running deliveries in Mumbai. Your dashboard says: “Heavy rain in Mumbai.” Do you stop the whole city? On 26 July 2005, Santacruz got 944 millimetres of rain. Colaba, 20 kilometres away, got 73. The problem isn’t weather data. It’s knowing what to do, zone by zone.

[Slide 2 | 0:19–0:37]
But Jio may already own infrastructure that can carry a signal about that rain. Rain weakens microwave signals between telecom sites. Measure the extra loss and you can estimate rain along each link. JioMausam combines that with radar and gauges into alerts businesses can act on.

[Slide 3 | 0:37–0:58]
Sounds great? We thought so too. The physics is well established, so we tested it ourselves: 500 real links from a public dataset in Germany, not Jio’s. The links tracked radar closely, but alone they caught only 68% of rain events. Our bar is 80%. That gap told us how to build JioMausam.

[Slide 4 | 0:58–1:15]
So we don’t bet on one sensor. Accuracy decides the mix: Jio’s links, network-health data to filter out faults, radar, gauges, satellite. The pilot tests every combination against held-out gauges. The answer isn’t “Jio only”. It’s the cheapest mix that works.

[Slide 5 | 1:15–1:32]
Before selling to Swiggy, where do we prove it? Inside Reliance, starting with Jio. A signal drops: is it rain, or is something broken? If JioMausam can answer that before a crew goes out, that’s real value. Then JioMart tests commercial value; MyJio tests reach.

[Slide 6 | 1:32–1:49]
Once that works, the same intelligence goes outside. Rain hits Zone A. JioMausam flags it. The platform decides whether to reroute, delay or raise incentives. That’s the product: not a prettier weather map, but a signal that changes a business decision.

[Slide 7 | 1:49–2:04]
So why Jio? Not because it invented the physics. The edge is integration: Jio’s signal, JioMausam’s intelligence, Reliance’s proving ground and reach. We can monetise the intelligence outside and create savings inside Reliance, even if insurance never launches.

[Slide 8 | 2:04–2:22]
Kavach is where we started: protection for workers. Then we stress-tested it. Who pays? Is the trigger accurate? Can an insurer price it? The insurance isn’t the business; the intelligence underneath it is. So Kavach becomes one application, only after the accuracy and actuarial gates pass.

[Slide 9 | 2:22–2:39]
If we offer it, Indian weather risk sets the starting point for the price. From 25 years of IMD records: about 45 rupees a month in Mumbai, 52 in Nagpur, 29 in Ahmedabad, and 208 for Mumbai Plus. All illustrative until actuarial validation.

[Slide 10 | 2:39–3:00]
We’re not asking you to believe us. We’re asking Jio to test it. Ninety days, three questions. Does Jio have the coverage? Does the combined system clear our bar? Does it improve a real Reliance operation? If yes, scale. If not, stop. First prove the signal. Then sell the intelligence. Protection comes last.

HOW THE 68% WAS CALCULATED (know this cold)

Tool: no AI or machine-learning model. Physics formulas plus simple statistics, run in Python.
  · pycomlink 0.6.0: the open-source Python toolbox for rain from commercial microwave links,
    built at Karlsruhe Institute of Technology (KIT), Germany. BSD licence.
    Code and data: https://github.com/pycomlink/pycomlink   (data folder: pycomlink/io/example_data)
    Install: pip install pycomlink   (https://pypi.org/project/pycomlink/)
  · From pycomlink we used three standard functions: dry baseline, wet-antenna correction (Schleiss et al. 2013),
    and the rain formula (ITU-R P.838, 2005 coefficients).
  · Our own code did the rest: cleaning, wet/dry detection, calibration, the zone map, and the yes/no comparison.
    File: prototype/pipeline/rain_pipeline.py (the Test Lab re-runs the same steps in a browser).

Data (all bundled in pycomlink's example data):
  · example_cml_data.nc: 500 real microwave links from a German mobile network, 2 directions each,
    signal levels every minute for 11 days (10-20 May 2018). Locations shifted for anonymity.
  · example_path_averaged_reference_data.nc: radar rain along each link, every 5 minutes.
  · example_areal_reference_data.nc: the radar rain map (190 x 228 squares of ~1 km, every 5 minutes),
    German Weather Service (Deutscher Wetterdienst) RADOLAN YW product. DWD open data: https://opendata.dwd.de

Steps:
  1. Signal loss = power sent - power received, every minute, every link.
  2. Wet or dry: if the loss wobbles much more than that link's normal, it is raining.
  3. Subtract the normal dry loss and a small wet-antenna allowance (up to 1.5 dB) = loss caused by rain.
  4. Turn rain loss into rain rate with the ITU formula (depends on link length, frequency, polarization).
  5. Average to hourly. One scale factor (1.17) tuned on 10-12 May only; 13-20 May never used for tuning.
  6. Spread link rain into ~1,800 zones of ~4 km (nearest links weigh more).
  7. Each zone, each of the 8 test days: did any 3 hours reach 10 mm? Ask the same of radar's map.
     14,256 yes/no decisions. Radar said yes 1,639 times; links alone also said yes 1,120 times.
     1,120 / 1,639 = 68%.

The papers behind the method:
  · Messer, Zinevich & Alpert, Science (2006): rain measured from a cellular network, Israel. doi:10.1126/science.1120034
  · Overeem, Leijnse & Uijlenhoet, PNAS (2013): country-wide rain maps of the Netherlands. doi:10.1073/pnas.1217961110
  · Schleiss, Rieckermann & Berne (2013): wet-antenna correction for microwave links.
  · ITU-R Recommendation P.838: the standard rain-to-signal-loss formula.

India data (prices): NOAA Global Summary of the Day, the public archive of IMD station reports.
  https://noaa-gsod-pds.s3.amazonaws.com/{year}/{station}.csv
  e.g. https://noaa-gsod-pds.s3.amazonaws.com/2024/43003099999.csv (Mumbai Santacruz)
  Stations: 43003099999 Santacruz · 42867099999 Nagpur · 42647099999 Ahmedabad · 43057099999 Colaba
